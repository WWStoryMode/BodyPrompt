# Day 4 — writing a poem, and finding where the body will travel

## 1. Where I started

Three days of putting other people's cues to the models. Today I wrote my own poem, iteratively,
in the instrument — the thing it was built for and the thing I had not yet done with it.

I started it as composition and it turned into an experiment, because the poem kept failing in
the same place — and then, in a second poem, stopped failing. **Thirteen bakes across nine
versions of two poems, two models**, one refusal that turned out to have a precise boundary.

**Kimodo only, to begin with.** It is the only model that can carry one line into the next
(`can_stitch_poems: true`); the other two generate each line from nothing. A poem is a score
where the body continues, so for most of today the comparison was Kimodo against itself.

## 2. The first poem, and a body that would not move

Five lines, 18 seconds, standing:

> Time is frozen in a liminal space. / Nobody knows how to make a move. / But changes happen
> slowly. / Slowly. / Slowly, but slowly noticeable.

The body was stiller than anything in three days of research. Articulation 0.008–0.013 m/s with
the pelvis removed; wrists moving one to ten centimetres across five seconds; almost every frame
below 3 cm/s of mean joint movement. Day 1's stillest result — *Budapest* — ran 0.06 m/s. This
was quieter than that by a factor of five.

**And the movement decreased line by line.** Left wrist: 10.4 cm → 4.3 → 4.1 → 2.4 → **1.4**. A
sevenfold decline.

My poem's arc runs the other way. It opens frozen and turns — *but changes happen slowly…
slowly noticeable*. The text moves toward change becoming perceptible; the body moved toward
less change. It answered the first half and inverted the second.

I could not tell from one bake whether that was the model reading "slowly" and "frozen"
literally, or the stitching losing energy line by line the way Day 1 found the pelvis losing
height. Both look identical in the measurement.

**One thing the instrument did to the poem, which I had not noticed until I measured it.** All
five durations were left blank, so they came from word count — and that gave *"Slowly."* the
**shortest** window in the poem, two seconds, the instrument's minimum. A poem about slowness,
written in deliberately short lines, gets the fastest tempo, because brevity of text is being
read as brevity of time. That is the instrument imposing a rhythm that contradicts the writing.

## 3. Rewriting toward the physical

I replaced tempo-only language with weight and shifting — *"But something begins to shift"*,
*"Slowly, the weight finds another place"* — and set durations by hand instead of letting word
count choose them. 18 seconds became 30.

It worked. The poem reached 0.068 m/s on its liveliest line with a wrist span of 0.89 m, an arm
genuinely travelling — roughly sixty times the stillest line of the first version. **And the
monotonic decline was gone**, replaced by up-and-down. So the first poem's fade was the language
or that seed, not a structural property of the stitching. The pelvis held at 0.980–0.984 and
drifted slightly *up*, where the first version drifted down.

**But which line moves is not determined by the writing.** I baked the same five prompts twice,
same durations, only the seed differing, and the liveliest line moved from four to three. Line
one is the clearest case: unchanged text, unchanged five seconds, and its wrist span across
bakes ran 0.104 m and 0.338 m — with a third bake, since overwritten, measuring 0.018 m.

So the writing sets *how much* the poem moves; the seed sets *where*. I would have claimed, from
one bake, that *"the weight finds another place"* produced a weight shift. The second bake says
that reading was luck.

## 4. Action description, and a body that finally sits down

Then I wrote the poem as explicit action: a person seated on the floor, rocking, beginning to
move, travelling across the floor, arriving and becoming still. Three seeds, fifty seconds each.

**The body went to the floor.** Pelvis 0.07–0.14 m against the previous versions' 0.97–0.98;
head at 0.65–0.67 in line one where it had been 1.57. Every earlier bake was a standing figure.
This is the largest change any rewrite produced, and it held across all three seeds.

**And line one became reproducible.** Wrist span across the three seeds: 0.006, 0.013, 0.007 — a
2.3× spread, where the poetic version's line one spread 19× across its bakes. Explicit
description converges; poetic language scatters. That is Day 3's rating finding appearing in the
poem instrument rather than in a spreadsheet, and it is the clearest corroboration I have.

## 5. What refuses

Line four asks the person to move across the floor. Net displacement:

| | 003a | 003b | 003c | 005 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| line 4 | 0.058 m | 0.012 m | 0.025 m | **0.011 m** |
| whole poem, 50 s | 0.445 m | 0.019 m | 0.068 m | 0.062 m |

One to six centimetres. In one seed the body ends **1.9 cm** from where it started after fifty
seconds.

**It fails by changing posture instead of position.** Head height drops at line four in every
seed — the body sinks, lies back, lifts its legs. Asked to displace itself it reorganises in
place. Root translation is the channel that does not answer; everything the request produces
comes out as limb and torso movement.

I tried the obvious fix and it failed informatively. Suspecting that *"moves from one place to
another"* states an outcome rather than a mechanism, I rewrote line four to name the mechanism —
*"places both hands on the floor beside the hips, pushes against the floor, and scoots the pelvis
forward"*. **The hands were already on the floor.** For 85–100% of that line's frames, across
all four bakes, under both wordings. Naming the push described what the model was already doing,
and that version travelled the least of the four.

So: the model produces the local kinematics of scooting and never produces what those kinematics
are for. It is the gesture of travelling, performed by a body that does not travel. Like someone
miming rowing.

**Stated as a hypothesis, because it was one.** Day 1 recorded Kimodo covering 2.11 m on a
locomotion phrase, so displacement is plainly available to it. What appeared unavailable was
*seated* displacement. **Sections 8 to 10 test that, and it survives** — but only after I had
first drawn the wrong boundary around it.

**And the return never happens.** Line five asks the body to arrive somewhere new and become
still again in the pose from line one. It does none of the three, and it is 20 to 33 times more
active than line one across the seeds. All three seeds fail this the same way — line five's wrist
spans cluster at 0.436, 0.477, 0.468, a 1.1× spread. **The model converges on ignoring "become
still again."** That is the sharpest form yet of Day 1's finding that the stitching has no
restoring force: the poem's own instruction to return is the one thing it reliably will not do.

## 6. The other model trades the opposite way

I brought SnapMoGen up and gave it the same five prompts. Every line restates *seated* — and it
is seated in **none** of them: head heights 0.155, 1.274, 1.426, 0.884, 1.412. It lies down for
the first line and stands for the rest.

But line four travelled **0.30 m**, where Kimodo managed 0.011–0.058 m. The channel that would
not answer for one model answers for the other.

**Kimodo obeys the frame and will not move; SnapMoGen moves and will not hold the frame.**
Neither gives a seated body crossing a floor.

Two caveats on that comparison, and the first is structural. SnapMoGen **cannot read a poem** —
`can_stitch_poems: false` — so what I ran were five independent drafts against Kimodo's single
continuous bake. SnapMoGen can stand up at line two *because line two starts from nothing*, while
Kimodo's line two inherits a seated body. Kimodo's seatedness may therefore be carry-over rather
than comprehension, and only its first line is unambiguously read from the prompt. **The control
that settles this — drafting the same five lines with Kimodo alone — has not been run.** Second:
SnapMoGen's feet pass 8 cm below the floor on line two, its GlobalRegressor still unwired.

I had predicted SnapMoGen would do better here, from Day 3's ratings — it was the only model
that climbed monotonically and the only one peaking at the explicit rung. **That prediction was
wrong**, and the reason narrows Day 3's finding usefully: those ratings were on Bausch cues,
which name an action and never a posture to *maintain*. A model that generates each line from
nothing has no mechanism for sustaining anything, however explicit the words. Day 3's result is
about whether a single line is legible, not about whether a poem holds together.

## 7. What I went looking for, and did not find

Knowing SnapMoGen's published design includes an LLM that rewrites expressive language into
detailed physical description before generation, I wanted to test the same prompts through it.

**It does not exist in this build**, and the service actively prevents one
(`providers.py:228`: *the worker may never rewrite the researcher's phrasing*). Day 2 recorded
this; today I confirmed it in the code rather than in a metadata field — which matters, because
the field I had been citing, `promptRewriteApplied`, **exists nowhere in the codebase**. My own
Day 2 driver hardcoded it `False`. The conclusion holds, but the evidence I had been quoting for
it was my own assertion.

Nor can Kimodo's local LLM be borrowed for the job. It is Llama-3-8B under LLM2Vec — no
`lm_head`, bidirectional attention, adapters trained for embedding, no `generate` method. It
pools hidden states into a conditioning vector. The base Instruct weights are on disk and can
generate, but at 16 GB they cannot sit beside Kimodo's 16–17 GB in 23 GB of RAM.

**Then the measurement that reframed it.** Words per prompt:

| | words |
|---|---|
| SnapMoGen's own published demo examples | 5.4 |
| my poem, version 1 (poetic) | 4.6 |
| Day 2 ladder — O | 4.6 |
| — Q | 19.1 |
| — A | 24.4 |
| — **E** | **38.7** |
| my poem, version 5 | 24.6 |

**My prompt ladder is a hand-made rewriter.** Day 2's finding — *the translation step is mine* —
is exactly the statement that I have been doing this job myself. And today's prompts were
already in the rewritten register, so the missing rewriter probably does not explain what I saw:
SnapMoGen was given in-distribution language and ignored the postural constraint anyway.

One thing that follows, and it is a caveat on the existing record: **Stage 1 calibration may have
tested SnapMoGen outside its own pipeline.** Those published examples average 5.4 words — user
style, not training style. If upstream would have rewritten them before generation, the
calibration ran the model on input its pipeline exists to prevent it from seeing.

## 8. A second poem, with no body in it

Late in the day I abandoned the seated poem and wrote a different one. Five lines, 35 seconds:

> In a split second, a match catches fire. / The floor loses its balance and rocks like a ship
> on a rough ocean. / Water runs across the planks, rushing left and right. / Water leaps high,
> trying in vain to reach a place to remain. / Water splashes all around like heavy rain.

**Not one line names a human body.** The subjects are a match, the floor, and water. By the
ladder's logic this is the O rung — imagistic, indirect, no action prescribed — and Day 3's
ratings put that rung at 0.73 out of 4.

It produced the most articulate body of the day so far: articulation 0.052–0.188 m/s against
the seated poem's 0.004–0.043 and the first poem's 0.008–0.013. Line 3 alone was four times the
liveliest line of the fully action-described poem. The arms went above the shoulder for 57% of
line 3 and 54% of line 5, peaking at 2.13 m; nothing else today raised them at all. Line 4 left
the ground — both feet clear, pelvis 0.847 to 1.083 — the first jump of the session.

My reading: **these images carry motor content without carrying a body.** *Runs across*,
*rushing*, *leaps high*, *splashes all around* are verbs of vigorous action, and the model
appears to retrieve on them and supply a body. Whereas *"the person uses subtle pushes and
weight shifts to move slowly"* names a body and an action, but the action is small — and small
is what it delivered.

Which suggests the variable predicting movement may not be specificity at all, but **how
energetic the verbs are**. The Day 2 ladder varies specificity while holding the referent
constant; it never varied verb energy independently. This poem did so by accident.

**And it still did not travel.** Net displacement 0.02–0.03 m per line, 0.020 m across the whole
35 seconds. Line 3 says *runs across the planks, rushing left and right* and the body stays put.

I concluded from that — wrongly — that the seated frame had been ruled out as the cause, since
here was a standing, vigorous, jumping body that also refused to travel.

## 9. Three translations, and the refusal breaks

Then I did to my own poem what Day 2 did to Bausch's: translated it up the ladder. Three
independent explicit versions, each naming the person and the action, averaging 26 words a line
against the imagistic version's 10.

| | |
|---|---|
| **006** | Water runs across the planks, rushing left and right. |
| **007a** | The person runs quickly toward the left, changes direction, and runs toward the right, repeatedly reversing direction with energetic arm swings and rapid footwork. |
| **007b** | The person travels across the floor in broad sideways movements, pushing strongly from one leg to the other… |
| **007c** | The person moves rapidly around the space with short running steps, sudden turns, and repeated changes of direction… |

**The body ran.** Path length on that line: 9.51 m, 4.12 m, 6.00 m — against 0.38 m for the
water version and 0.22 m for the seated scoot. Whole-poem net displacement 3.63, 1.07 and
3.61 m, where every previous version of the day managed centimetres. Articulation peaked at
0.632 m/s. In one bake the pelvis dropped to 0.41 m and rose to 1.15 with the lowest joint of
the body 0.88 m off the floor: an airborne phase, not a rise onto the toes.

**So my reading of the imagistic poem was wrong**, and the error is worth keeping. Poem 006 is
standing and vigorous and does not travel — but the thing travelling *in the sentence* is water,
not the person. The model gave the body water's **energy** and not its **displacement**. That is
a different failure from the seated one, and I had collapsed the two into a single refusal.

Three cases, not one:

| | subject | posture | travels |
|---|---|---|---|
| seated poem | a person | **seated** | no — 1–6 cm |
| water poem | **water** | standing | no — 0.03 m |
| explicit poem | a person | standing | **yes — 3.6 m** |

## 10. Replication

I re-baked all three explicit versions on fresh seeds, lines 1–4 byte-identical.

| line 3 wording | seed 1 | seed 2 |
|---|---|---|
| a — *runs quickly toward the left… the right* | 9.51 m | 6.81 m |
| b — *travels in broad sideways movements* | 4.12 m | 4.39 m |
| c — *moves rapidly around the space, short running steps* | 6.00 m | 8.04 m |

**Six bakes, three wordings, range 4.12–9.51 m, mean 6.48 m.** Every one of six.

The ordering is stable as well as the effect: **b travels least in both seeds**, and b is the
one describing sideways weight transfer rather than running. So the wording sets the magnitude
even where the seed sets the scatter.

Articulation replicates closely — 0.125/0.124 on line 1a, 0.491/0.521 on line 3a, 0.408/0.469
on 3b. **Jump height does not**: 0.88 → 0.62 m, 0.13 → 0.22 m. Real jumps both times, but the
height is not yet a reportable quantity.

And one wording fails *consistently*: version c's opening line — *"a quick, startled contraction,
immediately followed by a forceful…"* — gives 0.004 and 0.026 m/s, near-motionless in both. A
wording that fails the same way twice is more useful than one that fails at random.

Set against poem 002 this morning, where identical text swung **19×** in wrist span: explicit
high-energy prompts are far more seed-stable than poetic ones. That is the same convergence the
seated poem showed at the quiet end (2.3× against 19×), now visible at the loud end too.

## 11. Where this leaves the poem, and the research

The afternoon's finding was that the instrument refuses to travel. By evening it had become
something better — a boundary with a control on each side:

> **Root translation answers to explicit locomotion by an upright human body**, and to nothing
> else tested. Six bakes, three wordings, 4–9.5 m. Not a seated scoot (0.011 m). Not water
> running across planks (0.38 m). Not an outcome-worded traverse (0.012–0.058 m).

That is a positive claim rather than a complaint, and the path to it ran through an error of
mine that the second poem invited and the third corrected. Worth recording as such: the water
poem looked like it removed the seated explanation, and it did not, because I had stopped asking
*what is the subject of the sentence*.

Two smaller findings sit alongside it. **Articulation and displacement are separable channels** —
the water poem bought a great deal of the first and none of the second, which no single measure
would have shown. And **energy may matter independently of specificity**: the imagistic poem
outmoved the fully action-described seated poem by a factor of four, at a rung the ladder scores
lowest. If that survives testing it is a gap in the ladder's design, because the ladder cannot
separate the two.

What is owed:

- **Vary verb energy while holding specificity and referent fixed.** One line, two wordings —
  *water runs across the planks* against *water lies still across the planks*. It is the cheapest
  experiment available and the ladder cannot answer it.
- **Draft the seated five lines with Kimodo alone**, without the bake, to settle whether the
  seated frame was comprehension or inheritance. Still five minutes, still not done.
- **The caption comparison**, specified in [`../roadmap.md`](../roadmap.md): whether level E is
  already inside the language SnapMoGen was trained on.
- **A duration ceiling worth naming.** Every line of every explicit poem hit `MAX_SECONDS`, ten
  seconds. A body that now covers six metres in ten may simply need longer than the instrument
  can ask for.

Today generated no research corpus and no dataset. It produced one poem that fails, a second
that moves, an error I made in between, and a boundary that three wordings and six seeds agree
on — which is the first time the instrument has been used as an instrument rather than as an
apparatus.
