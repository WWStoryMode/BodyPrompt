# Day 2 — From poetic cue to movement instruction

## 1. Where I started today

Day 1 asked whether text-to-motion AI can generate dance or only body movement, and left a
sharper problem: the models retrieve where a dancer associates. Today's question is about
degree rather than kind. I have since watched the whole Stage 2 dataset; this entry is written
after that viewing, and it changed the framework more than the results.

**How descriptive does a choreographic prompt need to become before different text-to-motion
models produce a recognisable bodily response, and what is lost when poetic ambiguity is
progressively translated into explicit movement instruction?**

I want to stay careful about what these systems are. Not a collective human mind — a
statistical archive of associations between language, bodies, movement descriptions and
cultural representations of action that happened to be written down.

## 2. Three models, three relationships with language

**SnapMoGen / MoMask++ — interpretation.** In its published design, expressive language may
first be rewritten into detailed physical description before generation. That rewriting is
what I most wanted to watch, and it does not happen in my build — no rewrite stage exists in
the worker, so model prompt equals user prompt on every motion.

**Kimodo — composition.** Strongest as language becomes physically and spatially explicit.
Text-only throughout, to keep it comparable.

**Language of Motion — embodied language.** Relevant to gesture, body parts and non-verbal
communication. Restricted to text-to-motion.

Three instruments that read language differently; their disagreement is the measurement.

## 3. Stage 1 calibration

Before any choreographic material I calibrated each system with prompts from its own published
examples — the kind of sentence each was built to answer. Kimodo first, then the other two.
Twenty-three motions. Not artistic judgement: confirmation that BodyPrompt is talking to each
model correctly — right prompt in, valid motion out, durations honoured, seeds recorded. For
SnapMoGen I kept both user and model prompts; they are identical.

## 4. Stage 2 prompt ladder

Each cue was translated through five levels:

- **O — Original**
- **S — Semantic**
- **Q — Body quality**
- **A — Action description**
- **E — Explicit movement instruction**

Poetic / indirect → semantic meaning → bodily quality → observable action → explicit
instruction.

I built this expecting a threshold — a level at which each model starts answering.
**Watching the videos, I did not find one.** There is no point on the scale where
body movement becomes dance, or where a model begins to understand choreography.

What I see instead is a distribution of tendencies, depending on specificity but also on which
model, which kind of cue, which choreographer's way of transmitting movement, and on variation
between generations of the same prompt. One model may do something interesting with an
abstract cue while another needs bodily-quality language first. Specificity stays a useful
variable, but it is not a staircase and I should stop drawing it as one.

## 5. What I think I mean by dance

Watching several hundred generated bodies forced me to say what I am looking for. My working
proposition, loosely held:

> Dance movement is bodily movement that carries, associates with, or transmits an emotion,
> thought, sensation, intention or internal state through the body and its movement.

That state might be improvised, stimulated by a cue, reflected on, or transformed into the
dancer's own interpretation. Which is to say the dancer is not only executing choreography —
answering an indirect cue, they become a choreographic agent.

What matters for reading these videos: **visible complexity is not the test.** A small gesture
can carry a strong internal relationship; an elaborate sequence can stay functional and
mechanical. I have seen both. A practical lens, not a settled definition.

## 6. Experimental controls

Three variations per prompt; seeds 42, 1234 and 5678. The same user-facing text to all three
models, eight seconds throughout. No manual selection, no regeneration because a result looked
strange, no model-specific improvement, no Kimodo spatial constraints.

Most importantly, **the documented dancer response is withheld from generation entirely** — it
reaches the models through no channel. If I let a historical solution into a prompt and then
found the AI producing it, I would have discovered nothing but my own contamination. It is
comparison evidence, never ground truth.

## 7. Modes of transmission

Specificity does not describe how choreographers hand movement over. Four kinds of cue became
visible:

**Abstract** — an idea, emotion, memory or mental state, no body or environment specified.
*Trance*. *Desperate longing*. Joy and sorrow. The performer must build a bodily relationship
to something that begins as non-physical.

**Situational** — an image, environment, object, relationship or situation a body might be in.
Feet imagined glued to the floor; carrying someone; negotiating an apparatus or another
person. Concrete or figurative, but no movement specified.

**Somatic** — the body, its sensation, internal organisation or quality, no action prescribed.
Bones floating inside flesh; energy travelling from a centre; a quake cultivated in the
pelvis. The dancer is pointed at a state, not a sequence.

**Kinematic** — observable movement: raise an arm, turn sharply, three steps forward, follow a
curved path.

**These are not four more specificity levels.** They describe what kind of information a cue
carries — a separate dimension. A cue can be long and still situational, or four words and
kinematically explicit. Provisional, and I expect it to change.

Forti pushes toward a third dimension: **individual / internal body ←→ relational / material
world**. A somatic cue can be impossible to see yet possible for one body; a Forti score can
be plainly describable yet unperformable, because the apparatus and the other people are
missing. Different failures, which I had been treating as one.

## 8. Four choreographic practices

**Pina Bausch** — strong cases of abstract and situational transmission: *Trance*, *Something
with strength and energy*, *How would you like others to treat you?*, *Trevi Fountain*, *Sharp
turn*, *Desperate longing*. Almost no explicit movement information in any.

**Deborah Hay** — perceptual, attentional, paradoxical and spatial propositions: joy and
sorrow crossing the face without resolving; a wordless song generating movement; an unfamiliar
stride along a curved path; travelling straight while erasing the destination. Some describe
attention rather than visible movement. The joy/sorrow cue is a deliberate representational
stress test: SMPL-22 cannot encode facial expression, and none of the three models generated
face data.

**Ohad Naharin / Gaga** — the strongest somatic and imagistic cases: Lena, Biba, Oba, Ashi,
Tashi, bones floating inside flesh, both arms as one rope, a quake cultivated in the pelvis.
These ask for something internally sensed rather than executed.

**Simone Forti** — task, rule and relation: Huddle, Slant Board, Hangers, Roller Boxes,
Platforms, See Saw, Censor. Movement emerges from a physical situation rather than being
described. Her difficulty is distinct: the text can be entirely concrete while the model lacks
the world it needs — another body, a rope, a board, contact. I postponed Forsythe, whose most
relevant cases lean on visual and geometric information while this phase concentrates on
language as the input.

None sits in one category, and that crossing is why I chose them.

## 9. Dataset created

| practice | motions |
|---|---|
| Pina Bausch | 315 |
| Deborah Hay | 225 |
| Ohad Naharin / Gaga | 360 |
| Simone Forti | 315 |
| **total** | **1,215** |

Plus 23 calibration motions.

I expected the evidence to run *cue → prompt translation → model interpretation → generated
movement*. It does — but the translation step is **mine**. The ladder is my writing, and the
model-side rewriting I hoped to observe is absent. That absence is itself a result.

## 10. What watching showed, and the tool it demands

The viewing exposed a gap in the instrument. I can generate 1,215 motions in an afternoon and
have no way to record what I think of any of them — my judgement lives in my head and in
whatever I remember by evening, which is not evidence.

So the next step is an annotation tool inside BodyPrompt: watch a motion, rate it, keep that
beside it. **I have not settled the criteria** and would rather design them against the
footage. Candidate questions, not final metrics:

- Does this read as functional movement or as expressive movement?
- Does it appear to carry an internal thought, feeling, sensation or intention?
- Is there a perceptible relationship between the cue and what the body does?
- How distinctive is the interpretation, or how generic?

The point is to turn qualitative viewing into structured data, so distributions across
**model × specificity × transmission mode × rating** can be examined rather than asserted.

## 11. Where this leaves the research

The first qualitative viewing has happened. Systematic analysis has not, and I am not ranking
three models on an afternoon of watching. What changed is the framework: no single threshold
at which movement becomes dance; distributions across model, cue and specificity rather than a
staircase; specificity as one dimension beside mode of transmission; and a human-rating tool
needed before any stronger claim.

The open questions stay open — what is lost as language becomes machine-legible, whether more
description narrows interpretive diversity, which cues collapse into familiar gestures, and
how these bodies sit beside the documented human responses without treating the human as
correct.

The next step is to turn the act of watching into data — without losing the subjective,
artistic judgement that made the distinction visible in the first place.
